Dear Members of the Admissions Committee,

I am applying to the MSc in Physics at the University of Alberta for Fall 2027. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto and expect to graduate in June 2027. My research on galaxy kinematics and simulated Higgs events has developed my interest in computational physics and statistical inference. I would like to build on this experience by studying how astrophysical assumptions and detector effects enter the modelling and interpretation of dark-matter searches.

In 2025–2026, I independently investigated the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I built a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, using Markov chain Monte Carlo to preserve correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration. Prior-corrected importance sampling connected individual-galaxy posteriors to population-level inference, while prior-sensitivity analyses and sampling diagnostics helped me examine the model's behaviour. Under fixed photometric inclinations, I recovered a modest negative slope for the selected galaxies. However, subsample tests allowing for inclination uncertainty broadened the constraints. This limitation made me more interested in how observational and modelling assumptions influence what can be learned about dark matter. The project has led to a completed first-author manuscript that has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on kinematic feature selection for signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I compared multilayer perceptron classifiers trained on different feature groups through a common pyhf profile-likelihood analysis. Classifier AUC and signal-strength interval width did not always favour the same inputs. I used Shapley attribution to examine feature contributions, and bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics to assess the reliability of apparent precision gains. This exploratory work has strengthened my interest in evaluating an analysis through the physical parameter it helps constrain. A first-author manuscript is in preparation.

My undergraduate training in theoretical physics, laboratory work, and programming has provided a foundation for these projects. I am currently taking Computational Physics, Introduction to High Energy Physics, and Time Series Analysis, alongside other advanced physics courses. I now want to deepen the theoretical and numerical understanding needed to choose and justify physical models more independently.

For my MSc at Alberta, I would like to develop computational modelling and statistical analysis skills through research in astroparticle physics. I am interested in connecting predictions of dark-matter signals with the physical constraints obtained from experimental data. This requires a stronger understanding of recoil kinematics, detector response, and backgrounds, so that I can assess both the statistical behaviour of an analysis and the physical assumptions behind it.

I am particularly interested in Professor Marie-Cécile Piro's research on dark-matter detection, directionality, and detector microphysics. Her recent co-authored work on directional dark-matter signatures of the Large Magellanic Cloud connects astrophysical modelling to experimental predictions by examining how the local dark-matter distribution shapes recoil signals. This connection, together with her group's use of Monte Carlo simulation and data analysis, makes her research relevant to the questions I hope to pursue.

I would welcome a supervised modelling or analysis task suited to my Python and statistical-inference background. A possible starting point to discuss would be learning to reproduce directional recoil predictions for different dark-matter velocity distributions, then examining how detector response and background assumptions affect the inferred constraints. My MaNGA project provides experience in testing the sensitivity of inferred parameters to modelling assumptions, while my Higgs project provides experience with signal and background templates in a likelihood analysis. I would need to learn the detection physics and simulation methods required for this work.

My long-term goal is to pursue doctoral research using computational and statistical methods to investigate fundamental physics. Through Alberta's MSc, I hope to develop greater independence in formulating research questions and evaluating physical models, while contributing my experience in Python analysis and uncertainty assessment to a focused astroparticle-physics project.

Yours sincerely,

Hongyi Xu
