Hongyi Xu

Dear Members of the Admissions Committee,

I am applying to the MSc in Physics at the University of Alberta for Fall 2027. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto and expect to graduate in June 2027. My research on galaxy kinematics and simulated Higgs events has developed my interest in computational physics and statistical inference. I am particularly interested in learning how the methods used to infer physical parameters can contribute to experimental investigations of dark matter.

My undergraduate training in classical mechanics, electromagnetism, quantum mechanics, laboratory work, and programming has provided a foundation for this direction. Current courses in Computational Physics, Introduction to High Energy Physics, and Time Series Analysis are helping me relate the methods used in my research to a broader physical framework. I now want to extend this foundation through supervised research in astroparticle physics.

In 2025–2026, I independently investigated the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I built a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, using Markov chain Monte Carlo to preserve correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration. Prior-corrected importance sampling connected individual-galaxy posteriors to population-level inference, while prior-sensitivity analyses and sampling diagnostics helped me examine the model's behaviour. Under fixed photometric inclinations, I recovered a modest negative slope for the selected galaxies. However, subsample tests allowing for inclination uncertainty broadened the constraints. This limitation made me more interested in how observational and modelling assumptions influence what can be learned about dark matter. The project has led to a completed first-author manuscript that has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on kinematic feature selection for signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I compared multilayer perceptron classifiers trained on different feature groups through a common pyhf profile-likelihood analysis. Classifier AUC and signal-strength interval width did not always favour the same inputs. I used Shapley attribution to examine feature contributions, and bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics to assess the reliability of apparent precision gains. This exploratory work has strengthened my interest in evaluating an analysis through the physical parameter it helps constrain. A first-author manuscript is in preparation.

Together, these projects have given me experience in modelling and inference, while showing me the importance of understanding the underlying physics. For my MSc, I want to learn how a physical interaction becomes an observable signal, how detector response modifies that signal, and how backgrounds and systematic uncertainties enter an analysis. Further graduate study would help me connect statistical reasoning with these experimental questions and develop a focused research direction before pursuing a PhD.

At Alberta, I am particularly interested in Professor Marie-Cécile Piro's astroparticle-physics group. Her research on dark-matter detection, directionality detectors, and detector microphysics offers a concrete setting in which to develop this understanding. I am also interested in her group's work combining bubble-chamber technology with scintillation light, because it raises questions about how different observable responses can inform the interpretation of rare events. The group's use of Monte Carlo simulation and data analysis connects with my computational interests while offering substantial new physics and detector methods to learn.

I would hope to contribute initially through a supervised modelling or analysis task suited to my Python and statistical-inference experience. One question I would be interested in discussing is how assumptions about the dark-matter velocity distribution affect predicted directional recoil signals and the conclusions drawn from them. Pursuing such a question would require me to learn recoil kinematics, detector response, and relevant background processes. The connection to my MaNGA work lies in examining the sensitivity of an inference to its physical assumptions, while my Higgs project provides experience in working with signal and background templates within a likelihood analysis.

My long-term goal is to pursue doctoral research using computational and statistical methods to investigate fundamental physics. At Alberta, I hope to develop the physical understanding and research judgement needed to connect an analysis to an experimental question, while contributing the care in uncertainty assessment that I have begun to develop through my undergraduate work.

Yours sincerely,

Hongyi Xu
