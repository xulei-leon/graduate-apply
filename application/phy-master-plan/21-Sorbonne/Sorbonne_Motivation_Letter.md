# Motivation Letter for the Paris Physics Master

Hongyi Xu  
University of Toronto | hyi.xu@mail.utoronto.ca

Dear Members of the Admissions Committee,

I am applying to the M1 Paris Physics Master at Sorbonne Université for autumn 2027. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto and expect to graduate in June 2027. Through research on galaxy kinematics and simulated Higgs events, I have developed an interest in how computational methods can reveal the properties of physical systems, and how we can assess the reliability of those inferences. These projects have drawn me towards computational physics and statistical inference and motivated me to pursue further training in fundamental physics.

In 2025–2026, I independently investigated the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I built a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, retaining correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration through Markov chain Monte Carlo and connecting galaxy-level posteriors to population-level inference. Under fixed photometric inclinations, I recovered a modest negative slope for the selected galaxies, but allowing for inclination uncertainty in subsample tests broadened the constraints. This limitation taught me that a numerically stable analysis can still yield a physical conclusion that depends strongly on modelling assumptions. I became more interested in understanding which constraints came from the data and which depended on prior and model choices. The project has led to a completed first-author manuscript that has not yet been submitted.

My work in particle physics has extended this concern with model assumptions to the criteria used to evaluate an analysis. Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on kinematic feature selection for signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I compared multilayer perceptron classifiers trained on different feature groups through a common pyhf profile-likelihood analysis. Classifier AUC and signal-strength interval width did not always favour the same inputs. I used Shapley attribution to examine feature contributions, and bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics to assess the reliability of apparent precision gains. These exploratory results taught me to evaluate a model in relation to the physical parameter it helps infer, while checking whether narrower intervals are reliable. This has given my interest in machine learning for physics a clearer focus. A first-author manuscript is in preparation.

My undergraduate training in theoretical physics, laboratory work, and programming has provided a foundation for these projects. I am currently taking Computational Physics, Introduction to High Energy Physics, and Time Series Analysis, alongside other advanced physics courses. I now want to deepen the theoretical and numerical understanding needed to choose and justify physical models more independently.

The PPM's broad M1 training would support this goal before I choose an M2 specialisation. Advanced quantum mechanics and Statistical physics would deepen my theoretical foundations, while Numerical methods for physics would extend my computational training beyond the approaches used in my projects. The electives Astrophysics and cosmology and Nuclear and particle physics would let me study both research areas more systematically. This combination is valuable to me because my interests connect the two fields through inference methods, while each requires its own physical understanding.

Sorbonne's research environment offers further opportunities to explore these connections. Professor Benjamin Wandelt's work at the Institut d'Astrophysique de Paris on astrophysical data, machine learning, and statistical learning resonates with the questions about inference that emerged from my MaNGA project. At the LPNHE, Professor Bertrand Laforge's contributions to Higgs analyses in ATLAS and to machine-learning methods for electron and photon reconstruction interest me as examples of how computational choices enter experimental physics. I would welcome the opportunity to explore a related question during the compulsory research internship, bringing experience in modelling and uncertainty analysis while learning to develop a project through discussion within a research team.

My long-term goal is to pursue a PhD using computational and statistical methods in particle physics or astrophysics. I see the PPM as the next step towards that goal: a chance to broaden my physical understanding, make an informed choice of research direction, and contribute the independence and care I have developed through my projects.

Yours sincerely,

Hongyi Xu
