# Motivation Letter for the Master Year 1 Physics

Hongyi Xu  
University of Toronto | hyi.xu@mail.utoronto.ca

Dear Members of the Admissions Committee,

I am applying to the Master Year 1 Physics at Institut Polytechnique de Paris for autumn 2027. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto, with graduation expected in June 2027. Research on galaxy kinematics and simulated Higgs events has drawn me towards computational physics and the question of how reliably models support physical inference.

In 2025–2026, I independently investigated the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I developed a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, retaining correlated parameter uncertainties through Markov chain Monte Carlo and connecting galaxy-level posteriors to population-level inference. Under fixed photometric inclinations, I recovered a modest negative slope, while subsample tests allowing for inclination uncertainty broadened the constraints. This taught me to distinguish numerical stability from the robustness of a physical conclusion, and to examine how assumptions and prior choices influence what the data can establish. A first-author manuscript is complete but has not yet been submitted.

My particle-physics work has extended this concern to the criteria used to evaluate models. Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I compared multilayer perceptron classifiers trained on different feature groups through a common pyhf profile-likelihood analysis. Classifier AUC and signal-strength interval width did not always favour the same inputs. Shapley attribution, bootstrap resampling, and conditional coverage diagnostics helped me investigate feature contributions and apparent precision gains. These exploratory results strengthened my interest in evaluating computational methods through the physical inference they support. A first-author manuscript is in preparation.

My current coursework in Computational Physics, Statistical Mechanics, and Time Series Analysis supports this work. I now seek broader theoretical and numerical training to choose and justify physical models more independently. IP Paris's M1 Physics offers a useful combination: Numerical physics would extend my computational training, Astrophysics would deepen the context of my galaxy research, and Quantum physics of electrons in solids would introduce me to a different setting for modelling interacting systems. I value the possibility of combining courses from different majors, subject to the coordinator's approval, before choosing an M2 specialisation.

The Centre de Physique Théorique also interests me as a place to broaden my understanding of computational physics. Professor Silke Biermann's work on methods for describing strongly correlated electronic systems from first principles offers a setting in which I would like to learn how physical approximations and numerical methods are developed together. This would build on my concern with modelling assumptions while introducing me to new physical questions.

The opportunity to undertake laboratory research projects during coursework, followed by a four- to six-month research internship, is a central attraction. I would bring experience in building analyses and scrutinising uncertainties, while learning to formulate questions through sustained collaboration. My long-term goal is a PhD in computational physics; the M1 would help me develop the breadth and judgement needed for independent research.

Yours sincerely,

Hongyi Xu
