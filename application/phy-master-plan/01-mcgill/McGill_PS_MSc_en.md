# Personal Statement for the MSc in Physics (Thesis)

Hongyi Xu  
University of Toronto | hyi.xu@mail.utoronto.ca

Dear Members of the Admissions Committee,

I am applying to the Master of Science in Physics (Thesis) at McGill University for Fall 2027. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto and expect to graduate in June 2027. My research on galaxy kinematics and simulated Higgs events has drawn me towards computational physics and statistical inference. In both settings, I have become interested in how observations constrain physical models, and how those constraints change when we reconsider the assumptions behind an analysis.

My undergraduate training in classical mechanics, electromagnetism, quantum mechanics, laboratory work, and programming has provided a foundation for these projects. Current courses in Computational Physics, Introduction to High Energy Physics, and Time Series Analysis are helping me connect the methods I use with a broader understanding of the systems I study.

In 2025–2026, I independently investigated the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I built a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, using Markov chain Monte Carlo to retain correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration. I connected the galaxy-level posteriors to population-level inference through prior-corrected importance sampling and examined the analysis through prior-sensitivity tests and sampling diagnostics. Under fixed photometric inclinations, I recovered a modest negative slope for the selected galaxies, but subsample tests allowing for inclination uncertainty broadened the constraints. This limitation made me more attentive to the distinction between a stable numerical result and a well-supported physical interpretation. The project has led to a completed first-author manuscript that has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on kinematic feature selection for signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I compared multilayer perceptron classifiers trained on different feature groups through a common pyhf profile-likelihood analysis. Classifier AUC and signal-strength interval width did not always favour the same inputs. I used Shapley attribution to examine feature contributions, and bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics to assess the reliability of apparent precision gains. These exploratory results taught me to evaluate computational choices through the physical inference they support. A first-author manuscript is in preparation.

These experiences have also shown me where I need further training. I want to understand the physics behind the models more deeply, so that I can move beyond implementing an analysis towards choosing and justifying its assumptions. McGill's combination of graduate coursework and sustained thesis research would support this development. The department's course options in computational physics and particle physics are particularly relevant to the foundations I want to strengthen, while working within a research group would help me learn to refine a question through regular discussion and criticism.

I am particularly interested in Professor James Cline's work at the interface of particle physics and cosmology, including dark-matter models and their astrophysical consequences. My MaNGA project has made me curious about how halo observations can test explanations for dark matter beyond a phenomenological density profile. Under supervision, I would like to explore how uncertainties in inferred halo properties affect comparisons with physical models, while developing the particle-physics and cosmological knowledge needed to interpret those comparisons.

Professor Brigitte Vachon's work on high-energy particle collisions, including the McGill ATLAS group, offers another connection to my interests. My Higgs project has given me an initial understanding of how event representations enter parameter inference. I would welcome the opportunity to extend this experience through a focused analysis task and learn more about how detector response and background modelling influence the conclusions of a collider study. These interests share a concern with testing physical models through computation, and I would hope to develop one of them into a well-defined MSc thesis with an appropriate supervisor.

My long-term goal is to pursue a PhD using computational and statistical methods in particle physics or astrophysics. At McGill, I hope to deepen my physical understanding, develop a clearer research direction, and contribute the care in modelling and uncertainty analysis that I have begun to develop through my undergraduate projects.

Yours sincerely,

Hongyi Xu
