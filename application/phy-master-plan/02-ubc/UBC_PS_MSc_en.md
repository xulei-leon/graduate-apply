# Personal Statement for the MSc in Physics

Hongyi Xu  
University of Toronto | hyi.xu@mail.utoronto.ca

Dear Members of the Admissions Committee,

I am applying to the Master of Science in Physics at the University of British Columbia for Fall 2027. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto and expect to graduate in June 2027. Through research on dark-matter halos and simulated Higgs events, I have become interested in how computational and statistical methods extract physical information from data. I want to understand both what an analysis can constrain and how confidently those constraints can be interpreted.

In 2025–2026, I independently studied the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I built a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, retaining correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration through Markov chain Monte Carlo. I then used prior-corrected importance sampling to connect individual-galaxy posteriors to population-level inference. Prior-sensitivity analyses, sampling diagnostics, and inclination tests helped me examine the limits of the model. Although the selected galaxies showed a modest negative concentration–mass slope under fixed photometric inclinations, allowing for inclination uncertainty in subsample tests broadened the constraints. This experience made the reliability of inferred halo properties a central research interest. A first-author manuscript is complete but has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on kinematic feature selection for signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I compared multilayer perceptron classifiers trained on different feature groups through a common pyhf profile-likelihood analysis. Classifier AUC and signal-strength interval width did not always favour the same inputs. Shapley attribution helped me examine feature contributions, while bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics showed why apparent precision gains require further scrutiny. This exploratory project has taught me to judge machine-learning methods by the physical inference they support. A first-author manuscript is in preparation.

My undergraduate training in theoretical physics, laboratory work, and programming has provided a foundation for these projects. I am currently taking Computational Physics, Introduction to High Energy Physics, and Time Series Analysis, alongside other advanced physics courses. I now want to deepen the theoretical and numerical understanding needed to choose and justify physical models more independently.

UBC's Physics MSc combines a research thesis with graduate coursework selected in consultation with a supervisor. This structure would allow me to deepen my understanding of a particular physical system while extending my computational and statistical training. The department's research in both astrophysics and particle physics is especially relevant to the two areas in which I have begun to work.

Professor Ludovic Van Waerbeke's research on gravitational lensing, dark matter, and structure formation connects closely with the questions that emerged from my MaNGA project. I would like to learn how lensing provides constraints on mass distributions and how assumptions about halo structure and environment enter those inferences. My experience with correlated posteriors and sensitivity tests would provide a starting point for learning these methods. A possible question to develop under supervision would be how alternative halo-model assumptions affect mass recovery in simulated lensing data.

I am also interested in Professor Alison Lister's work in ATLAS, particularly the use of machine learning in searches for physics beyond the Standard Model. My Higgs analysis has made me curious about how choices of classifier inputs and background models propagate into a final physical constraint. I would welcome a supervised analysis project in which I could build on my experience with profile likelihoods and uncertainty diagnostics while learning the experimental context and validation required for a collider search. These two directions offer different physical settings in which to develop my interest in reliable inference; I would work with a supervisor to choose a focused thesis question.

My long-term goal is doctoral research in particle physics or astrophysics with computation and quantitative analysis at its centre. I see UBC's Physics MSc as an opportunity to develop stronger physical judgement through sustained research and to contribute my experience in modelling, statistical analysis, and careful examination of uncertainty.

Yours sincerely,

Hongyi Xu
