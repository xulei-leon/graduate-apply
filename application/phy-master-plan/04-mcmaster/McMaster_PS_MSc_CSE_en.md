# Personal Statement for the M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering (Thesis)

Hongyi Xu  
University of Toronto | hyi.xu@mail.utoronto.ca

While comparing classifiers for simulated Higgs events, I found that classification AUC and the width of a subsequent parameter interval did not always favour the same inputs. This discrepancy drew my attention to how computational methods should be evaluated when they form part of a larger scientific analysis. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto, with graduation expected in June 2027. I am applying to the thesis M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering at McMaster University for Fall 2027 to study the methods underlying such analyses more systematically.

My physics training has given me practice translating questions about a system into mathematical models and examining the assumptions that make those models tractable. Programming courses and research in Python have helped me turn these models into analyses that I can test and revise. I am currently taking Computational Physics and Time Series Analysis to strengthen this foundation. Working across astronomical observations and particle simulations has also encouraged me to look for shared computational questions, particularly how uncertainty enters a model and how its effects can be assessed.

In 2025–2026, I independently investigated the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I developed a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, using Markov chain Monte Carlo to retain correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration. To connect individual-galaxy posteriors to population-level inference, I used prior-corrected importance sampling and checked the stability of the reweighting. This required attention to how assumptions made at one stage affected the next. Prior-sensitivity analyses and sampling diagnostics helped me examine the model, while subsample tests allowing for inclination uncertainty broadened the constraints on the relation. I learned to distinguish numerical convergence from the strength of the information available in the data. The project made me more interested in methods for tracing uncertainty through successive modelling stages. A first-author manuscript is complete but has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on feature attribution for signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated signal and background samples, I organised 19 kinematic variables into four groups and compared all 15 nonempty combinations with multilayer perceptron classifiers across five training seeds. I built a common pyhf profile-likelihood workflow so that the feature sets could be compared through the same inference procedure. Exact Shapley attribution helped me examine their contributions, and event-group bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics helped assess the reliability of apparent improvements. These checks showed why narrower nominal intervals alone could not establish a calibrated precision gain. The work remains exploratory, with a manuscript in preparation. It has taught me to connect the design of a machine-learning comparison to its scientific objective and to examine what an evaluation can actually support.

These experiences have given me practical skills, but they have also clarified what I need to learn. I want a stronger grounding in numerical methods, optimisation and statistical modelling so that I can justify choices I have so far learned largely through particular projects. I am especially interested in scientific machine learning: how learned representations can be incorporated into computational models, and how their usefulness can be tested against the quantities we ultimately want to infer. During a master's thesis, I hope to become more capable of formulating these questions, comparing approaches and designing validation procedures before interpreting results.

McMaster's Computational Science and Engineering program appeals to me because it brings scientific applications together with computational simulation, modelling and optimisation across science and engineering. Computational Physical Sciences would provide a natural starting point for my background, while the broader interdisciplinary setting would expose me to problems with different assumptions and validation needs. I want to understand which lessons from my current analyses transfer to other settings and which depend on the application. The two-year M.Sc. thesis route would allow me to pursue that development through a sustained research project under supervision. One direction I would like to explore is how uncertainty in data and model choices propagates through an analysis that combines learned features with parameter estimation. A focused thesis problem would help me turn this broad interest into a tractable investigation with clearly defined comparisons and tests.

My long-term goal is to pursue research in scientific computing and prepare for doctoral study in a related field. I would bring experience building Python analysis workflows, carrying uncertainty through statistical inference and investigating the limits of computational results. At McMaster, I hope to develop the mathematical and numerical judgement to move from implementing an analysis to explaining why its methods are appropriate, where they fail and how they can be improved.
