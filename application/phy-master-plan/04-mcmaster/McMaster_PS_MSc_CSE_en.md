# Personal Statement for the M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering

Hongyi Xu  
University of Toronto | hyi.xu@mail.utoronto.ca

I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto, with graduation expected in June 2027. Through research on galaxy dynamics and simulated Higgs events, I have developed an interest in computational modelling, statistical inference and uncertainty analysis. I am applying to the M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering at McMaster University for Fall 2027 to strengthen my mathematical and numerical foundations and learn to design and evaluate scientific computing methods more systematically.

My research on the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation introduced me to the difficulties of carrying uncertainty through successive modelling stages. Using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies, I built a Python and PyMC workflow combining Bayesian MCMC with population-level inference. In subsample tests, allowing for uncertainty in galaxy inclination broadened the inferred constraints, showing me how a modelling choice could limit a scientific conclusion. A first-author manuscript is complete but has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on signal-strength inference using public ATLAS simulated Higgs events. I compared groups of kinematic inputs using neural-network classifiers and a common pyhf profile-likelihood workflow. Feature attribution, bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics helped me assess what apparent improvements could support. This exploratory work, with a manuscript in preparation, has made me particularly interested in evaluating machine learning against the scientific quantities we ultimately want to infer.

I now want to strengthen my grounding in numerical methods, optimisation and statistical modelling. My projects have taught me to implement and test analyses, but I want a more systematic basis for choosing methods and understanding their limitations. I am especially interested in how learned representations affect parameter estimation when data are limited or model assumptions are uncertain. Under supervision, I would like to study a focused version of this problem using simulated data with known parameters, comparing estimation error and uncertainty across alternative representations. Defining a manageable question, choosing informative baselines and planning validation before implementation are skills I hope to develop through graduate study.

McMaster's CSE program appeals to me for its emphasis on computational simulation, modelling and optimisation, and its collaboration across science, engineering and business. Computational Physical Sciences connects directly with my background, while the broader setting would help me examine which methods transfer across applications and which depend on the underlying problem. I want to bring mathematical coursework and practical computation together in a substantial piece of supervised work, developing both a documented implementation and a careful account of its performance. I would welcome refining my proposed direction around a supervisor's expertise and learning from applications beyond those I have studied.

My longer-term goal is to contribute to research in scientific computing. At McMaster, I hope to build the mathematical breadth and independent judgement needed to formulate useful computational questions, evaluate competing approaches and explain the limits of their results.
