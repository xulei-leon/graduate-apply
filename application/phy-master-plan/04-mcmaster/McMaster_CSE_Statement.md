# Statement of Interest

Hongyi Xu | McMaster University | M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering

I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto, with graduation expected in June 2027. Through research involving observational galaxy data and simulated Higgs events, I have developed an interest in computational modelling, statistical inference, and numerical analysis. I am applying to McMaster University’s M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering for Fall 2027 to strengthen my mathematical foundations and learn to design, implement, and evaluate computational methods more systematically.

My research on the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation introduced me to the challenges of building reliable computational workflows. Using data from 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies, I developed a Python and PyMC pipeline combining Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo with population-level inference. Beyond implementing the analysis, I examined how parameter correlations and modelling assumptions affected the results. In subsample tests, accounting for uncertainty in galaxy inclination broadened the inferred constraints. This experience taught me that a computational model must be evaluated not only by whether it produces a result, but also by how sensitive that result is to its assumptions. A first-author manuscript based on this work is complete but has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on neural-network selection and statistical analysis of simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulations, I construct kinematic variables and compare classifiers with different input groups, separating training, validation, and evaluation samples. I examine how event selection affects expected signal and background yields and four-lepton mass distributions, and use profile-likelihood analysis and bootstrap resampling to investigate statistical uncertainty. This ongoing project is developing my interest in evaluating computational methods within a defined physical analysis, including how input choices affect parameter estimation and uncertainty.

Through these projects, I have gained experience implementing computational pipelines, analysing complex datasets, and testing the sensitivity of results. I now want a more systematic understanding of numerical methods, optimization, and statistical modelling so that I can make better-informed decisions when selecting algorithms and designing validation procedures. I am particularly interested in how computational representations affect parameter estimation under limited data or uncertain model assumptions. One direction I would like to explore is comparing alternative representations using simulated datasets with known parameters, evaluating their estimation error, uncertainty calibration, and robustness. I would develop a focused research question under supervision, with clear baselines and validation criteria established before implementation.

McMaster’s M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering appeals to me because it connects computational modelling, simulation, and optimization across disciplines. Computational Physical Sciences offers a natural connection to my experience. The core courses, CSE 700, Foundations of Scientific Computing, and CSE 701, Foundations of Modern Scientific Programming, would help me connect mathematical reasoning with the implementation of computational methods. I want to apply this training to the choices I encounter in research: selecting algorithms, checking numerical behaviour, and designing reproducible comparisons. Through supervised research, I hope to refine my interest in parameter estimation into a focused project, while learning how methods transfer across scientific applications.

After graduation, I intend to pursue research-oriented opportunities in computational science, with the possibility of continuing to doctoral study. In the longer term, I hope to contribute as a research scientist to the development of computational methods for complex, data-intensive problems. McMaster would help me build the mathematical breadth, programming skills, and independent judgement needed to formulate meaningful computational questions, compare competing approaches, and assess how reliably their results can be used.
