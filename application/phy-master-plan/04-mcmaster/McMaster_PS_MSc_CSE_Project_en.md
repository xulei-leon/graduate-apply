# Personal Statement for the M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering (Project)

Hongyi Xu  
University of Toronto | hyi.xu@mail.utoronto.ca

Building analyses for galaxy observations and simulated particle events has shown me how much scientific computing depends on connecting individual methods into a coherent workflow. Preparing inputs, fitting a model and evaluating its outputs each involve choices that affect the final interpretation. I want to become better at making those choices and carrying a computational problem from formulation through implementation to validation. I am completing an Honours Bachelor of Science in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto, with graduation expected in June 2027, and am applying to the M.Sc. in Computational Science and Engineering at McMaster University through the Project option for Fall 2027.

My undergraduate training has provided a foundation in mathematical modelling, physical reasoning and programming. Research in Python has given me practice translating models into calculations, investigating unexpected behaviour and revisiting assumptions when results change. I am currently taking Computational Physics and Time Series Analysis to strengthen my numerical and analytical preparation. These experiences have made me interested in learning methods across disciplinary boundaries and understanding how to select an appropriate approach for a particular problem.

In 2025–2026, I independently studied the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies. I built a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, using Markov chain Monte Carlo to retain correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration. I then used prior-corrected importance sampling to connect the individual-galaxy posteriors to population-level inference. This work required me to consider the compatibility of successive analysis stages and check the stability of the reweighting. Prior-sensitivity analyses, sampling diagnostics and inclination-sensitivity tests became essential parts of interpreting the results. In particular, allowing for inclination uncertainty in subsample tests broadened the constraints on the relation. The experience taught me to treat validation as part of building an analysis, with assumptions and limitations made explicit alongside its outputs. A first-author manuscript is complete but has not yet been submitted.

Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on feature attribution for signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I grouped 19 kinematic variables into four sets and compared all 15 nonempty combinations with multilayer perceptron classifiers across five training seeds. I built a common pyhf profile-likelihood workflow to evaluate how the inputs affected parameter inference. Classification AUC and interval width did not always favour the same inputs, which made the choice of evaluation criterion central to the comparison. I used exact Shapley attribution, event-group bootstrap resampling and conditional coverage diagnostics to examine feature contributions and the reliability of apparent precision gains. This exploratory work, with a manuscript in preparation, strengthened my ability to organise a controlled computational comparison and report what its results could support.

I now want to broaden the numerical and statistical foundations behind this practical experience. My immediate priority is to study numerical methods, optimisation and scientific machine learning more systematically, then bring them together in a focused computational project. I am particularly interested in learning how to set a manageable scope, choose informative baselines and define evaluation criteria before implementation. The Project option fits this stage of my development because I want an opportunity to integrate newly learned methods into a complete piece of work. I would aim to produce a documented implementation, a careful comparison of approaches and a clear account of their limitations.

McMaster's Computational Science and Engineering program is attractive to me for its interdisciplinary connection between science and engineering and its emphasis on computational simulation, modelling and optimisation. Computational Physical Sciences offers a natural link to my background, while the broader setting would help me understand how computational choices change across applications. Within the 16-month M.Sc. Project route, I would like to explore a bounded comparison of feature representations for parameter estimation using simulated data with known parameters. Starting from a simple baseline, I could examine how choices affect estimation error, uncertainty and sensitivity to limited data, with the scope refined through guidance. This is a possible project interest that grows from my experience, and I would welcome other applications with similar methodological questions. The opportunity to connect broader learning with a concrete implementation is central to my interest in this route.

My longer-term goal remains research in scientific computing, with doctoral study as a possible next step once I have developed a clearer methodological direction. Through this master's program, I want to build the breadth and practical judgement needed to contribute to computational research across application areas. I would bring experience in Python modelling, statistical inference and systematic evaluation, together with a willingness to learn the mathematics and domain knowledge that a new problem requires.
