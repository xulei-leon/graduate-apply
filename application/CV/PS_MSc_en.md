My research in galaxy kinematics and particle physics simulations has drawn me to a central question in computational physics: how can we use data to constrain physical models and assess the reliability of those constraints? What appeals to me is the interplay between physical understanding and computation: we turn physical ideas into models we can test, then use the results to revisit our assumptions. I am pursuing an Honours Bachelor of Science (HBSc) in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto, expect to graduate in June 2027, and am applying for a master's program in physics beginning that autumn.

My undergraduate training in physics, laboratory work, and programming has prepared me to translate theoretical questions into computational analyses. Through modelling and data analysis in Python, I have learned to interpret results in light of model assumptions, parameter uncertainty, and numerical stability. I am currently enrolled in a Computational Physics course to develop a more systematic understanding of numerical methods and the conditions under which they are appropriate.

In 2025–2026, I independently completed a study of the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using MaNGA galaxy kinematics and wrote a first-author manuscript, which is complete but has not yet been submitted. The question was how well kinematic observations confined to the inner regions of a halo could constrain its mass and concentration. In early rotation-curve fits, different initial values could lead to different parameter combinations that fitted the velocity data similarly well. A single best fit could not convey this degeneracy. I therefore built a dynamical model of the stellar components and an NFW dark-matter halo in Python and PyMC, using MCMC sampling to retain the joint posterior distribution. Model development involved repeated checks of convergence, posterior geometry, predictive residuals, and sensitivity to prior choices. I then used prior-corrected importance sampling to propagate individual-galaxy posteriors into population inference, avoiding double-counting the first-stage prior and checking whether a few samples dominated the reweighting. This sustained process of modelling and checking taught me that posterior sampling can reveal a degeneracy without supplying information absent from the observations. Numerical convergence must also be considered alongside model assumptions and predictive checks before drawing physical conclusions. The experience deepened my interest in understanding how much our knowledge of halo structure comes from the data and how much depends on prior and model choices.

Since May 2026, I have worked under Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on kinematic features and signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS simulated samples, I compare feature combinations in classifiers that omit the four-lepton invariant mass as an explicit input, evaluating their contributions through a fixed likelihood procedure and Shapley attribution. Some compact input sets yield narrower nominal intervals than the full set, but event-group bootstrap analysis has not established this advantage, and conditional coverage diagnostics do not support a calibrated precision gain. These results identify compact candidates for further validation and have strengthened my interest in how machine-learning performance translates into reliable constraints on physical parameters.

Together, these projects have motivated me to study computational physics further, particularly how computational methods extract information about physical systems and how we judge that information's reliability. This training would help me choose research questions with greater understanding and prepare for doctoral study.
