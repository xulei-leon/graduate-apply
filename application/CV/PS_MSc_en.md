My research in galaxy kinematics and particle physics simulations has drawn me to a central question in computational physics: how can we use data to constrain physical models and assess the reliability of those constraints? What appeals to me is the interplay between physical understanding and computation: we turn physical ideas into models we can test, then use the results to revisit our assumptions. I am pursuing an Honours Bachelor of Science (HBSc) in the Physics Specialist program at the University of Toronto, expect to graduate in June 2027, and am applying for a master's program in physics beginning that autumn.

My undergraduate training in classical mechanics, electromagnetism, quantum mechanics, laboratory work, and programming has prepared me to translate theoretical questions into computational analyses. Through modelling and data analysis in Python, I have learned to interpret results in light of model assumptions, parameter uncertainty, and numerical stability. I am currently taking Computational Physics, Introduction to High Energy Physics, and Time Series Analysis, alongside further coursework in theoretical physics, to strengthen the numerical and physical foundations of my research.

In 2025–2026, I independently studied the dark-matter halo concentration–mass relation using 620 selected MaNGA disk galaxies and wrote a first-author manuscript, which is complete but has not yet been submitted. I developed a rotation-curve mass-decomposition pipeline in Python and PyMC, using MCMC to retain the correlated uncertainties in halo mass and concentration. I then used prior-corrected importance sampling to link individual-galaxy posteriors to population-level inference of the relation's slope, normalization, and intrinsic scatter. This required accounting for the first-stage priors and using PSIS diagnostics to assess the reliability of the reweighting. Prior predictive checks, prior-sensitivity analyses, and sampling diagnostics helped me examine the assumptions and numerical behaviour of the model. Under fixed photometric inclinations, I recovered a modest negative concentration–mass slope for the selected galaxies. However, subsample tests showed that allowing for inclination uncertainty broadened the constraints, identifying it as a major limitation of the analysis. This result shaped how I interpret computational findings: a numerically stable estimate can still depend strongly on assumptions about the observations. The project deepened my interest in understanding how much our knowledge of halo structure comes from the data and how much depends on prior and model choices.

Since May 2026, I have worked remotely with Professor Mario Campanelli at University College London on how kinematic feature selection affects signal-strength inference in simulated Higgs four-lepton events. Using public ATLAS H → ZZ* → 2e2μ signal and background samples, I grouped 19 kinematic variables into four feature sets and evaluated all 15 nonempty combinations with MLP classifiers across five random seeds. I built a unified pyhf profile-likelihood pipeline to compare classification performance with constraints on the Higgs signal strength, then applied exact Shapley attribution, 200 event-group bootstrap replicates, and conditional coverage diagnostics to assess feature contributions and inference reliability. Classifier AUC and signal-strength interval width did not always favour the same inputs. This finding reinforced the need to evaluate machine learning through the physical inference it supports and to check the reliability of apparent precision gains. A first-author manuscript is in preparation. The project has strengthened my interest in connecting computational methods to well-defined physics questions.

Together, these projects have motivated me to study computational physics further, particularly how computational methods extract information about physical systems and how we judge that information's reliability.
